Summary

ATSolver treats solver configuration as a sequential decision under accuracy, runtime, and memory constraints. A pretrained language model with fixed parameters proposes one typed configuration at a time over a portfolio of classical solvers and preconditioners. Trusted numerical software alone checks legality, runs the solver, recomputes the residual on the original system, and accounts for resources. Under matched budgets the controller accepts more runs than Best-of-N sampling, selects faster configurations, and invests in larger setups as the number of right-hand sides grows.

Abstract

Sparse linear solver configuration is a sequential decision problem under numerical, resource, and workload constraints. Each attempted configuration reveals execution evidence that can inform subsequent choices, while setup cost, solve cost, accuracy requirements, and search budgets jointly shape the tuning objective. We introduce ATSolver (Agentic Tuning of Solvers), a verifier-grounded framework for agentic sparse-solver tuning that iteratively proposes, executes, and revises configurations over a typed portfolio of classical sparse solvers and preconditioners. A pretrained language model with fixed parameters serves as the proposal policy, while trusted numerical software controls legality, execution, solution verification, resource accounting, and numerical-state reuse. Under matched six-proposal budgets in an iterative-solver study, ATSolver accepts 75/105 runs versus 64/105 for independent Best-of-6 and selects configurations with 30.1% lower independently replayed solve time on matrices solved by both policies in all repetitions. Across repeated-RHS workloads, ATSolver shifts toward greater setup investment as batch size grows, adapting configuration choices to changing setup–solve trade-offs. Feedback memory studies show that trial history avoids observed repeated proposals and that adding retrieved experience to knowledge improves configuration quality on held-out parameters within two PDE families. These results demonstrate effective agentic tuning under explicit accuracy, runtime, and memory constraints without updating the underlying language model.

1Motivation

Solver configuration is often framed as a decision for one matrix–right-hand-side pair \((A,b)\). In practice the numerical problem comes with requirements: a tolerance, time and memory limits, available hardware and software, and a workload that may contain one solve, a batch of right-hand sides sharing a matrix, or a sequence of related systems. These requirements interact. A setup that is too expensive for one solve may pay for itself over a batch, and a fast preconditioner may exceed the memory limit.

Every attempted configuration also produces evidence: whether it converged, the residual it reached, how long setup and solve took, and how much memory it used. ATSolver asks whether a language model can use this evidence to revise its next choice under a small, explicitly accounted budget, while numerical correctness stays under trusted control. A convergence flag, a model explanation, or a retrieved case never counts as acceptance on its own.

Contributions
  • Workload formulation. Configuration is posed as a feasibility-first sequential decision over single systems, batches of right-hand sides, and related-system sequences, with search cost and deployment cost accounted separately.
  • Verifier-grounded controller. A typed interface separates language-model proposals from numerical authority: software owns legality, execution, residual verification, resource accounting, the incumbent, and numerical state.
  • Controlled evaluation. Matched-budget studies of configuration quality, within-search history and retrieved experience, setup investment across batch sizes, and matrix-image inputs, with every failed and rejected proposal kept in the accounting.

2Problem setting

Three workload regimes are considered. Regime \(\mathsf{S}\) is a single linear system, regime \(\mathsf{M}\) is a batch of right-hand sides sharing one matrix, and regime \(\mathsf{Q}\) is a sequence of related systems whose operator may change between solves. For \(\mathsf{S}\) and \(\mathsf{M}\),

\[A x_k = b_k,\quad k=1,\dots,K,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\ \text{sparse},\qquad \mathcal{W}=(L,R,H,B)\]

where \(K=1\) in \(\mathsf{S}\) and \(K>1\) in \(\mathsf{M}\), and the workload contract \(\mathcal{W}\) collects

\(L\)
the regime and the characteristics of the linear systems
\(R\)
accuracy and coverage requirements
\(H\)
available hardware and solver capabilities
\(B\)
resource and search budgets

A configuration \(a\) specifies a solver, a preconditioner, numerical parameters, and permitted preprocessing or setup-reuse operations. Within the budget, the goal is first to find a configuration that satisfies the complete workload, and then to minimize the specified cost.

Numerical acceptance

Software independently verifies each computed solution \(\hat{x}_k\) by recomputing its residual on the original system:

\[\rho_k=\frac{\lVert A\hat{x}_k-b_k\rVert_2}{\max\{\lVert b_k\rVert_2,\ \epsilon_b\}}\ \le\ \tau_k\]

Here \(\epsilon_b>0\) guards against a zero or near-zero right-hand side. Every required right-hand side must meet its tolerance, and acceptance also requires finite outputs, a valid method, and compliance with the time and memory limits.

Search limits and cost

The controller may submit at most \(B_{\mathrm{prop}}\) proposals and execute at most \(B_{\mathrm{exec}}\) configurations. Every submission counts toward the proposal limit, including invalid, repeated, or unaffordable configurations and premature Stop requests. Only proposals that pass validation are executed. With \(g_j=1\) when proposal \(j\) is executed, the total numerical search cost is

\[C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}=C_{\mathrm{card}}+\sum_{j:\,g_j=1}C^{\mathrm{num}}_j\]

where \(C_{\mathrm{card}}\) is the one-time cost of preparing the workload summary and \(C^{\mathrm{num}}_j\) includes preprocessing, setup, solves, verification, and permitted state transfer. Successful and failed executions both contribute. The work to first acceptance, \(C_{\mathrm{first}}\), is the same sum up to the first accepted trial, and is infinite if none is accepted.

Deployment cost

The deployment cost is the time needed to run the selected configuration on the workload, excluding the search that found it:

\[C_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a;K)=C_{\mathrm{pre}}(a)+C_{\mathrm{setup}}(a)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}C_{\mathrm{solve},k}(a)+C_{\mathrm{transition}}(a)\]

When a setup is reused across right-hand sides, a larger initial investment may be repaid by cheaper subsequent solves. The batch study tests whether configuration choices follow this trade-off.

3Method

Decision inputs and actions

Before proposal \(j\), the model receives the decision state

\[d_j=\big(c_j,\ m^{\mathrm{arm}}_j,\ \mathcal{A}_j,\ \beta_j\big),\qquad a_j\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d_j)\]

where

\(c_j\)
workload information: dimensions, nonzero counts, structural and scaling properties, numerical estimates, requirements, and solver capabilities; matrix views and retrieved records where enabled
\(m^{\mathrm{arm}}_j\)
the trial history that the experimental condition exposes
\(\mathcal{A}_j\)
the legal actions
\(\beta_j\)
the remaining budgets

Matrix names, file paths, private numerical objects, and verifier state are withheld. Each action selects a solver and preconditioner, sets their parameters, and specifies permitted preprocessing, initial-guess, and setup operations. The portfolio contains PCG, GMRES, FGMRES, and BiCGSTAB with AMG, ILU, FSAI, or no preconditioner, plus serial SuperLU where permitted. Proposals use a typed interface restricted to implemented workflows, and the model never generates solver code.

Execution evidence and revision

An executed trial returns its acceptance status, residuals, phase timings, and memory measurements; a proposal rejected before execution returns its rejection reason. Rejected proposals are never repaired automatically. The model may revise parameters within the current solver–preconditioner combination or switch to another. After a failure, a deterministic procedure summarizes the failure type and the aspects that may need revision, without choosing the next solver. After a success, setup and solve measurements guide further proposals under the cost objective.

Incumbent and numerical state

Software keeps a verified incumbent and replaces it only when another accepted candidate meets the improvement rule; later failures leave it unchanged. Trial history and numerical state are kept apart. Each single-system candidate starts from the same prescribed state, and each batch candidate must solve the entire batch on its own, although within one candidate a setup may be built once and reused across right-hand sides. For related-system sequences, only the selected accepted candidate may pass validated state to the next system.

4Experimental setup

Data and implementation. 35 sparse matrices, 29 from SuiteSparse and six assembled with MFEM, spanning 32 families. Three model repetitions per workload give 105 runs per condition. Related matrices stay in the same partition, and matrix names are withheld from the model. The proposal policy is a fixed gpt-5.6-terra configuration, with native HYPRE 3.1 and serial SuperLU 7 in float64 on CPU. The primary contracts use \(\tau=10^{-8}\), full coverage, a 1 GiB memory limit, a 30 s per-action timeout, and a 60 s cumulative numerical deadline.

  1. RQ1, configuration quality. ATSolver against Direct and Expert-guided Best-of-3 at three proposal and three execution slots, with one-shot variants and a fixed GMRES+AMG configuration as single-configuration references. A separate six-proposal, iterative-only study compares ATSolver with independent Best-of-6; selected configurations receive one warm-up and five measured replays.
  2. RQ2, feedback memory. Within a search: no action history, tried configurations only, scalar outcomes, or structured diagnostics, under matched limits. Across workloads: knowledge alone (K) against knowledge plus retrieved trial experience (K+E), on held-out parameters within the Helmholtz and SUPG families.
  3. RQ3, setup investment. Batch sizes \(K\in\{2,8,32\}\), with every right-hand side required to pass. Controls are an expert amortization rule, a trained configuration ranker, and model-based search. All have three slots, but the controls choose among five preset configurations while ATSolver also tunes parameters.
  4. Matrix views. On eight matrices, no images against two structural views and four combined structural and signed-value views, with six proposals and three repetitions.

Metrics. Accepted counts numerically accepted runs, with failed searches included and rejected proposals charged to the budget. 1st-Accept Work is the restricted mean numerical work to first acceptance over a 180 s horizon. Full search time includes model and orchestration latency. Deployment includes preprocessing, setup, the required solves, and any prescribed state transfer, and excludes verification and search. Paired timings use jointly accepted runs, and a time ratio \(r\) corresponds to a \(100(1-r)\%\) reduction.

Within-search memory. No repeated proposal occurs in any condition that exposes tried configurations, against 94 of 315 proposals (29.8%) without action history. Because the gate charges rejected duplicates to the proposal budget, this memory keeps slots from being spent on configurations already tried. Final acceptance is 77/105 without history and 78/105 in every history condition. In a separate constructed-failure intervention, in which every parent is an unpreconditioned GMRES trial with a two-iteration limit, structured diagnostics give fewer accepted next actions than scalar outcomes, 62/105 against 68/105.

Retrieved experience. The target requires numerical acceptance and a solve time within \(1.05\times\) the best stable fixed recipe. With experience, 88.1% of searches reach it on the first proposal and all of them within three, against 31.0% and 42.9% for knowledge alone. Independent replay of the final selections gives 38/42 against 19/42. Both conditions satisfy all 42 numerical contracts, so the gain concerns solve performance rather than additional acceptance. Experience reduces paired replayed solve time by 11.0% and numerical-worker time by 44.5%, while increasing tokens by 36.2%.

At \(K=32\), on the 50 jointly accepted runs across 17 matrices, the configurations selected by ATSolver reduce full-batch deployment time by 48.5% relative to the expert rule (95% CI \([22.8,\,67.9]\)). Model-based search matches ATSolver’s 72/105 at that batch size, and the deployment reduction against it is 2.2% (95% CI \([-9.9,\,10.8]\)). On the fixed set of 72 pairs, direct-factorization selections rise from 22 to 55 as \(K\) grows from 2 to 32. Median setup time rises from 0.0338 to 0.2152 s, while median solve time per right-hand side falls from 0.03595 to 0.00772 s. Because the evaluated cases are held fixed, the shift is not explained by a change in the accepted subset: the controller spreads a more expensive setup over more solves. Its full search time is higher than the expert rule’s at every \(K\), and deployment savings exclude this overhead.

Related-system sequences. Over four-position sequences, joint control accepts 72/105 under each operator schedule, against 63/105 for carryover with prescribed rebuilding when the operator changes at every transition and 69/105 under mixed transitions.

5.4 Matrix views

All three image conditions accept 18/24 runs on eight matrices. On the six jointly successful matrices, two sparsity views, in the original and the reverse Cuthill–McKee orderings, reduce replayed solve time by 14.8% relative to no images. Four structural and signed-value views give a smaller 9.6% reduction. Both image conditions increase online search time.

6Ongoing extension: reinforcement learning

Every result above keeps the language model fixed: the controller improves within a search through feedback and memory, never through parameter updates. The extension now in progress trains the proposal policy itself with reinforcement learning, using the same trusted boundary as the source of reward.

The formulation carries over directly. A workload defines an episode, the decision state \(d_j\) is the observation, a typed configuration \(a_j\) is the action, and the per-proposal audit ledger records complete trajectories. The reward comes only from the verifier, never from the model’s own judgment, and follows the same feasibility-first ordering as the evaluation: acceptance of the complete workload first, then deployment cost, with search work charged against it. A reward of this form is

\[r(\tau)=\mathbf{1}\{\text{workload accepted}\}\Big(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{dep}}\,\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a^\star)\Big)-\lambda_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\,\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\]

where \(\tau\) is one search trajectory, \(a^\star\) the verified incumbent, \(\tilde{C}\) normalized costs, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{dep}},\lambda_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\ge0\) trade-off weights.

Because legality, execution, and verification remain outside the model, a learned policy cannot be rewarded for an unverified claim of success, and invalid or repeated proposals are charged exactly as they are now. The questions this extension targets are whether a trained policy reaches acceptable configurations in fewer proposals than the fixed model with memory, and whether it learns setup–solve trade-offs that transfer across batch sizes and matrix families.
