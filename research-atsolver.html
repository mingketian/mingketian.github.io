1Problem and significance

A workload consists of \(K\) sparse linear systems that share one operator,

\[A x_k=b_k,\quad k=1,\dots,K,\qquad A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\ \text{sparse},\qquad B_{\mathrm{rhs}}=[b_1,\dots,b_K]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K},\]

with \(K=1\) in the single-system regime \(\mathsf{S}\) and \(K>1\) in the batch regime \(\mathsf{M}\). A third regime \(\mathsf{Q}\) is a sequence of related systems whose operator may change between solves. The workload contract \(\mathcal{W}=(L,R,H,B)\) fixes the regime and system characteristics \(L\), the accuracy and coverage requirements \(R\), the hardware and solver capabilities \(H\), and the resource and search budgets \(B\). A configuration \(a\) chooses a solver, a preconditioner, their numerical parameters, and permitted preprocessing and setup-reuse operations.

Acceptance is decided only by recomputing each residual on the original system:

\[\begin{aligned}\rho_k(a)&=\frac{\lVert A\hat{x}_k(a)-b_k\rVert_2}{\max\{\lVert b_k\rVert_2,\ \epsilon_b\}},\\ \mathrm{acc}(a)&=\mathbf{1}\big\{\rho_k(a)\le\tau_k\ \ \forall k\big\}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\text{finite, valid, within time and memory limits}\},\end{aligned}\]

where \(\epsilon_b>0\) guards near-zero right-hand sides. The cost of using a configuration on the workload, excluding the search that found it, is

\[C_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a;K)=C_{\mathrm{pre}}(a)+C_{\mathrm{setup}}(a)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}C_{\mathrm{solve},k}(a)+C_{\mathrm{transition}}(a).\]

The configuration problem is therefore feasibility-first and budgeted: within at most \(B_{\mathrm{prop}}\) proposals and \(B_{\mathrm{exec}}\) executions, find an accepted configuration, then prefer the cheapest one,

\[a^\star\in\arg\min_{a\,:\,\mathrm{acc}(a)=1}\ C_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a;K)\qquad\text{subject to}\quad \#\text{proposals}\le B_{\mathrm{prop}},\ \ \#\text{executions}\le B_{\mathrm{exec}}.\]
  • The objective depends on the workload. The \(C_{\mathrm{setup}}\) term in Eq. (3) is paid once while the solve term grows with \(K\), so an expensive setup that is wasteful for one solve can be optimal for a batch.
  • The quantities that matter are observed only after execution. Convergence, the achieved residual, phase timings, and memory are revealed by running a candidate. Configuration is a sequential decision in which each trial is evidence for the next.
  • Correctness cannot be delegated. A learned proposer may be wrong or may claim success. Acceptance in Eq. (2) must stay with trusted numerical software, and every proposal, including invalid, repeated, or failed ones, must be charged to the budget.

2Research questions

The three properties above define what a controller must do: use execution evidence to revise proposals within a fixed budget, retain useful information across trials and searches, and adapt the setup–solve trade-off to the workload. A fourth question concerns the input representation of the matrix.

3Method

ATSolver separates proposal from authority. A pretrained language model \(\pi_\theta\) with fixed parameters chooses what to try; numerical software decides whether a proposal is legal, executes it, verifies the result, charges its cost, and keeps the best verified configuration. Algorithm 1 gives one search.

Algorithm 1  ATSolver search for one workload

Input: contract \(\mathcal{W}\); limits \(B_{\mathrm{prop}},B_{\mathrm{exec}}\); policy \(\pi_\theta\) (fixed).  Output: verified incumbent \(a^\star\) or a reasoned failure.

  1. build the workload card \(c\) from \(A\), \(B_{\mathrm{rhs}}\), \(\mathcal{W}\); charge \(C_{\mathrm{card}}\)  ▷ names and paths withheld
  2. \(a^\star\leftarrow\varnothing\);  \(m\leftarrow\varnothing\);  \(C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\leftarrow C_{\mathrm{card}}\)
  3. for \(j=1,\dots,B_{\mathrm{prop}}\):
  4. \(d_j\leftarrow(c,\ \mathrm{proj}(m),\ \mathcal{A}_j,\ \beta_j)\);  \(a_j\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d_j)\)  ▷ Eq. (5)
  5. if \(a_j=\textsf{STOP}\) and the stop rule admits it: break
  6. \(\eta_j\leftarrow\mathrm{gate}(a_j,\mathcal{A}_j)\); if rejected: \(m\leftarrow m\cup\{(a_j,\eta_j,\delta_j)\}\); continue  ▷ still charged to \(B_{\mathrm{prop}}\)
  7. execute \(a_j\) from the prescribed numerical state; observe \(e_j\); \(C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\leftarrow C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}+C^{\mathrm{num}}_j\)  ▷ Eq. (6)
  8. verify every residual (Eq. 2); if failed: \(\delta_j\leftarrow\mathrm{diagnose}(e_j)\)
  9. update \(a^\star\) by the replacement rule (Eq. 7); \(m\leftarrow m\cup\{(a_j,e_j,\delta_j)\}\); stop if \(B_{\mathrm{exec}}\) is exhausted
  10. return \(a^\star\), the full ledger \(m\), and \(C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\)

3.1Decision state and policy

Before proposal \(j\), the model sees the workload information \(c_j\), the trial history \(m^{\mathrm{arm}}_j\) permitted by the experimental condition, the legal action set \(\mathcal{A}_j\), and the remaining budgets \(\beta_j\):

\[d_j=\big(c_j,\ m^{\mathrm{arm}}_j,\ \mathcal{A}_j,\ \beta_j\big),\qquad a_j\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid d_j).\]

The card \(c_j\) contains the dimension \(n\), \(\mathrm{nnz}\), structural and scaling properties, trusted symmetry and SPD evidence, the accuracy and resource requirements, and the available solvers. Where enabled, it is extended by matrix views (sparsity in natural and reverse Cuthill–McKee order, positive and negative magnitudes) and by retrieved records. Matrix names, file paths, private numerical objects, and verifier state are withheld. The same \(\theta\) is used for the first proposal and for every revision; adaptation happens through \(d_j\), not through parameter updates.

3.2Typed action space

An action is a complete typed configuration over a fixed portfolio. The model does not write solver code; each proposal must contain exactly the required fields of its branch, and trusted checks restrict which branches are legal for a given matrix.

3.3Gate, execution, and search cost

Every submission counts toward \(B_{\mathrm{prop}}\), including invalid, duplicate, unaffordable, and premature \(\textsf{STOP}\) proposals. Only proposals that pass the schema and legality gate are executed and count toward \(B_{\mathrm{exec}}\). With \(g_j=1\) if proposal \(j\) is executed, total numerical search work is

\[C_{\mathrm{ctrl}}=C_{\mathrm{card}}+\sum_{j:\,g_j=1}C^{\mathrm{num}}_j ,\]

where \(C^{\mathrm{num}}_j\) covers preprocessing, setup, solves, verification, and permitted state transfer, for successful and failed trials alike. Work to first acceptance, \(C_{\mathrm{first}}\), sums the same terms up to the first accepted trial and is infinite if none is accepted. Model and orchestration latency are reported separately as full search time.

3.4Verification and the incumbent

An executed trial returns its acceptance status, residuals, phase timings, and memory; a rejected proposal returns its rejection reason. After a failure, a deterministic diagnostic summarizes the failure type and the aspects that may need revision; it does not choose the next solver. Software keeps the best accepted configuration under a fixed study objective \(J\):

\[a^\star_j=\begin{cases}a_j & \mathrm{acc}(a_j)=1\ \text{and}\ J(a_j)<(1-\kappa)\,J(a^\star_{j-1}),\\ a^\star_{j-1} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}\]

with \(J=C_{\mathrm{deploy}}\) and \(\kappa=0\) (strict improvement) in the three-proposal and batch studies, and \(J=\) solve time with \(\kappa=0.05\) in the six-proposal, image, and retrieval studies. Later failures cannot remove an incumbent. Each candidate starts from the same prescribed numerical state: trial history is shared, but setup objects and iterates are not.

3.5Memory: history projections and retrieved experience (RQ2)

The experimental condition fixes the projection \(\mathrm{proj}(m)\) of the ledger that enters \(d_j\). Four within-search projections form a ladder:

\[\mathcal{H}_0:\ \varnothing\ \ \subset\ \ \mathcal{H}_1:\ \{a_i\}_{i<j}\ \ \subset\ \ \mathcal{H}_2:\ \{(a_i,\ \text{scalar outcome}_i)\}_{i<j}\ \ \subset\ \ \mathcal{H}_3:\ \{(a_i,\ e_i,\ \delta_i)\}_{i<j},\]

that is, no action history, tried configurations, configurations with scalar outcomes, and configurations with the structured verifier record. All four keep the shared diagnostic handoff after a failure. Across searches, the card can be extended by solver knowledge \(\mathsf{K}\) alone or by knowledge plus retrieved trial records from earlier workloads, \(\mathsf{K}{+}\mathsf{E}\); retrieved choices must still pass the current verifier.

3.6Batches and operator sequences (RQ3)

In \(\mathsf{M}\), a candidate must solve the entire batch, and solves from different candidates cannot be combined. Within one candidate a setup is built once and reused, so the per-right-hand-side cost is

\[\frac{C_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a;K)}{K}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{pre}}(a)+C_{\mathrm{setup}}(a)+C_{\mathrm{transition}}(a)}{K}+\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}C_{\mathrm{solve},k}(a),\]

and the optimum of Eq. (4) shifts toward configurations with larger setup and smaller per-solve cost as \(K\) grows, for example from a Krylov method with a light preconditioner to a sparse direct factorization. The batch size is part of \(c_j\), so the controller can act on this trade-off. In \(\mathsf{Q}\), a sequence of four systems with operator shifts is solved under one 7/7 budget, and only the selected accepted candidate may pass validated numerical state (setup, iterate) to the next system, subject to checks when the matrix changes.

4Experimental protocol

The benchmark has 35 sparse matrices from 32 families: 29 from SuiteSparse and 6 assembled with MFEM. Three model repetitions per workload give 105 runs per condition. The proposal model is gpt-5.6-terra; the numerical stack is HYPRE 3.1 and serial SuperLU 7. Unless stated otherwise, \(\tau=10^{-8}\) with full coverage, a 30 s per-action timeout, a 60 s worker deadline, and a 1 GiB memory cap.

6Ongoing extension: reinforcement learning

All results above keep \(\theta\) fixed: the controller improves within a search through \(d_j\), never through parameter updates. The extension in progress trains the proposal policy with reinforcement learning, keeping the trusted boundary of §3 as the only source of reward.

Formulation. One workload is one episode, and the search of Algorithm 1 is a finite-horizon decision process

\[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{W}}=\big(\mathcal{D},\ \mathcal{A},\ P,\ r,\ B_{\mathrm{prop}}\big),\qquad d_{j+1}\sim P(\cdot\mid d_j,a_j),\]

whose state is the decision input \(d_j\) of Eq. (5), whose action space is the typed portfolio of Table 2 together with \(\textsf{STOP}\), and whose transition is realized by the gate, the numerical worker, and the verifier. The verifier ledger records every trajectory \(\xi=(d_1,a_1,e_1,\dots)\), including rejected and failed proposals. The reward follows the same feasibility-first order as Eq. (4):

\[r(\xi)=\mathrm{acc}(a^\star)\,\Big(1-\lambda_{\mathrm{dep}}\,\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{deploy}}(a^\star;K)\Big)-\lambda_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\,\tilde{C}_{\mathrm{ctrl}}(\xi),\qquad \max_{\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{W}}\,\mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(\xi)\big],\]

where \(a^\star\) is the verified incumbent at the end of the episode, \(\tilde{C}\) are costs normalized per workload, and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{dep}},\lambda_{\mathrm{ctrl}}\ge0\). Because legality, execution, and verification stay outside the model, a trained policy cannot be rewarded for an unverified claim of success, and invalid or repeated proposals are charged to the budget exactly as in the evaluation above.

Questions. Whether a trained policy reaches accepted configurations in fewer proposals than the fixed model with memory (Tables 4 and 7); whether it learns the setup–solve trade-off of Eq. (9) and transfers it across batch sizes and matrix families; and how it compares with retrieved experience (Table 9) as a way of carrying information across workloads.
