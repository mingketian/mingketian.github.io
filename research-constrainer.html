CONSTRAINER: Promptable Graph-Structured Optimization via Constraint Conditioning

Zeeshan Memon, Mingke Tian, Xinyuan Song, Hongwei Jin, Kibaek Kim, Liang Zhao

Department of Computer Science, Emory University · Argonne National Laboratory

TL;DR

Learned solvers usually bake their constraints into the network, so every reformulation needs a new model. CONSTRAINER parses each constraint into an operator graph, embeds it with a contrastively trained GNN, and lets a shared graph transformer attend to those embeddings. One model then follows whatever constraint set it is given, and transfers to new constraint compositions with little or no finetuning.

Abstract

Neural methods for graph-structured optimization perform well within individual problem classes but are typically trained for fixed formulations, which limits reuse across related problem variants. Recent foundation-style models share backbones across tasks, yet they mainly share instance-level representations and treat feasibility constraints implicitly. A key source of shared structure across constrained optimization problems is the constraint set itself, which overlaps substantially across tasks. We propose CONSTRAINER, a constraint-conditioned framework that treats constraints as structured inputs: constraint specifications are parsed into operator graphs and encoded by a graph neural network into invariant constraint embeddings, which condition a shared backbone for solution prediction. On power-grid optimization and mixed-integer linear programming, CONSTRAINER improves solution optimality and enables efficient transfer and generalization compared with baseline methods.

1Motivation

Real graph-structured systems such as power grids, logistics networks, and supply chains are re-optimized constantly. Most reformulations do not change the graph, the decision variables, or the objective; they add, remove, or tighten constraints as operating conditions, regulations, or reliability criteria change. Across tasks, feasible regions are governed by a small number of recurring constraint families: conservation laws and capacity limits in physical networks, and assignment, flow, and scheduling constraints in combinatorial problems.

This makes the constraint set a natural unit of reuse. Building a single promptable model around it raises three challenges:

  1. Relational structure. Constraints encode dependencies among variables and nodes; flattening them into vectors discards that structure.
  2. Equivalent formulations. The same constraint can be written many ways (rescalings, rearranged terms), so the model must be invariant to surface syntax.
  3. Composition. Constraint information must be interleaved with the graph and objective so that the model adapts to new constraint combinations it has not seen.
Contributions
  • A constraint-conditioned foundation model with a structured encoder that parses constraints into operator graphs and learns invariant embeddings.
  • A conditioning backbone that generalizes across constraint families and supports compositional transfer between reformulated tasks.
  • A stability guarantee: admissible reparameterizations of a constraint change its embedding by a bounded amount.
  • Experiments on power-grid optimization and MILP benchmarks showing better solutions and more efficient transfer than existing baselines.

2Problem formulation

A graph-structured constrained optimization problem is a triple of a graph, a constraint set, and an objective. Decision variables live on the nodes and edges of the graph; the constraints decide which assignments are feasible.

\[P=(G,\,C,\,f),\qquad X=\{\,x\in\Omega \mid c(x)=\text{true}\ \ \forall c\in C\,\},\qquad x^\star\in\arg\min_{x\in X} f(x)\]

where

\(G=(V,E)\)
the problem graph, for example buses and transmission lines, or cities and roads
\(C\)
explicit equality, inequality, and domain constraints (including integrality)
\(\Omega\)
all assignments of values to the decision variables on \(G\)
\(X\)
the feasible set induced by \(C\)

The goal is one model \(F_\theta\) that reads both the graph and the constraint specification and returns a high-quality, feasible solution:

\[\hat{x}=F_\theta(G,\,C)\]

3Constraint encoder: operator graphs

Each constraint \(c\) is written as the computation graph of its expression, a directed acyclic graph \(A_c=(V_c,E_c)\). Leaves are variables and constants; internal nodes are operators such as \(+\), \(\mathrm{pow}\), and \(\le\). The line-flow limit \(P^2+Q^2\le S_{\max}^2\), for example, becomes a small tree rooted at \(\le\). Nodes are initialized by type and refined by \(L\) rounds of message passing:

\[h_v^{(0)}=\begin{cases}\phi_{\mathrm{var}}(v) & \text{variable}\\ \phi_{\mathrm{const}}(v) & \text{constant or bound}\\ \phi_{\mathrm{op}}(v) & \text{operator}\end{cases}\qquad h_v^{(\ell+1)}=\psi^{(\ell)}\!\Big(h_v^{(\ell)},\ \textstyle\sum_{u:(u,v)\in E_c}\phi^{(\ell)}\big(h_u^{(\ell)},e_{uv}\big)\Big)\]

A permutation-invariant attention pooling turns the node states into one constraint embedding, \(z_c=\mathrm{POOL}\{h_v^{(L)}\}\in\mathbb{R}^d\).

Contrastive invariance

To make \(z_c\) reflect what a constraint allows rather than how it is written, the encoder is trained contrastively. Positives \(c^+\) are admissible rewrites (rescaling, rearranging the operator graph); negatives \(c^-_k\) look similar but change feasibility:

\[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{con}}(c)=-\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\mathrm{sim}(z_c,z_{c^+})/\tau\big)}{\exp\!\big(\mathrm{sim}(z_c,z_{c^+})/\tau\big)+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\exp\!\big(\mathrm{sim}(z_c,z_{c^-_k})/\tau\big)}\]

with cosine similarity \(\mathrm{sim}\) and temperature \(\tau\). The framework covers any constraint that reduces to a finite computation DAG over its operator vocabulary.

4Constraint-conditioned backbone

Instance features (power, capacity, demand, …) are embedded as typed feature tokens that share the variable-type embedding \(\phi_{\mathrm{var}}\) with the constraint encoder, so a symbol such as \(P\) means the same thing in the instance and in the constraint. A heterogeneous graph transformer (HGT) then runs in two halves. The first \(L/2\) layers reason over the problem graph; a cross-attention block lets every variable read the constraint set \(Z_C=\{z_c\}_{c\in C}\):

\[\tilde{h}_i=\sum_{c\in C}\alpha_{ic}\,W_v z_c,\qquad \alpha_{ic}=\operatorname*{softmax}_{c}\big\langle W_q h_i^{(L/2)},\,W_k z_c\big\rangle,\qquad h_i^{(L/2)}\leftarrow h_i^{(L/2)}+\tilde{h}_i\]

The remaining \(L/2\) layers spread the constraint-conditioned information across structurally related variables. Because constraints enter only through this attention, adding, removing, or replacing a constraint changes the prompt, not the network.

Task decoder

For continuous problems a shared MLP reads out every variable in parallel, \(\hat{x}_i=g_\theta(h_i)\). For discrete problems a masked autoregressive decoder takes a few steps and excludes constraint-violating actions. Training is supervised on optimal solutions with penalty terms for constraint violation.

5Theory: stability under reparameterization

Let \(A_{c,r}\) be the type-\(r\) adjacency matrix of the operator DAG of \(c\), and measure how far an admissible rewrite \(c'\) moves the graph by \(\delta=\max_r\lVert A_{c,r}-A_{c',r}\rVert_2\).

Theorem (informal)

Under a linear message-passing instantiation with bounded weights and bounded typed adjacencies, the embedding shift is bounded by a quantity that grows linearly in \(\delta\):

\[\lVert z_c-z_{c'}\rVert_2\;\le\;\lVert S\rVert_2\,\lVert \mathrm{vec}(H^{(0)})\rVert_2\,\big(a+bwR\Delta\big)^{L}\cdot\frac{L\,bwR\,\delta}{a+bwR\Delta}\]

Structurally closer rewrites therefore move the embedding less, and the contrastive objective explicitly tightens the bound in practice. A coverage argument in the appendix extends this to a generalization bound across constraint families.

Setup

Power grids. Power flow (PF), AC optimal power flow (ACOPF), DC optimal power flow (DCOPF), DC optimal transmission switching (DCOTS), and DC transmission network expansion planning (DCTNEP), generated with PowerModels.jl and solved with IPOPT (nonlinear) or HiGHS (mixed-integer). MILP. Routing, scheduling, packing, graph, and location problems on 100-node instances (JSSP 10×10, UMSP 100×20); 8 tasks for training and 8 held out for finetuning. Baselines. CANOS and a GAT-based OPF model (single-task), a multi-task graph transformer with task adapters, and neural combinatorial solvers including AM, POMO, Sym-NCO, BQ-NCO, MatNet, MVMoE, RouteFinder, OPTFM, MTL, and GOAL.

Against the multi-task models (OPTFM, MTL, GOAL), CONSTRAINER has the lowest gap on every routing task and on UMSP and JSSP. Gains are largest where feasibility depends on several interacting constraints (routing with time windows, scheduling) and small on packing and graph problems dominated by a single constraint family. On the grid, CONSTRAINER has the highest feasibility on every formulation, with the largest margins on DCOTS and DCTNEP, which couple discrete topology decisions with power-flow constraints.

Removing constraint conditioning degrades every task. Replacing the operator-graph encoder with text embeddings of the constraint (text-embedding-3-small) recovers part of the gap but stays well behind, which suggests that surface-level semantics do not preserve the compositional structure the DAG encoder captures. Injecting the cross-attention at the middle of the backbone (\(L/2\)) works best.

8Conclusion and limitations

Separating what the problem is (the graph) from what is allowed (the constraints) turns a learned solver into a promptable one. Encoding constraints as operator graphs gives invariant, reusable representations with a provable stability property, and conditioning a shared backbone on them improves solution quality, feasibility, and transfer.

Limitations. Gains are modest when a single constraint family dominates (packing, graph problems). Predictions are not guaranteed feasible on continuous problems; when strict ACOPF feasibility is required, the model is paired with a downstream refinement step (for example FSNet’s L-BFGS stage). The constraint vocabulary is limited to expressions that reduce to a finite operator DAG.
